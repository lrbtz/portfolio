Trustworthy Accessibility Baseline

To understand how accessibility was being addressed across projects, a colleague and I surveyed 42 initiatives and identified gaps in how it was measured and verified. Based on the research findings, we developed a practical checklist for designers, developers, and QA teams and applied it to a pilot project. The research was later formalized into a paper proposing a WCAG Level A baseline for digital accessibility.

Overview

Digital products are meant to work for everyone, with or without disabilities, yet organizations rarely turn that commitment into a practice they can check. This practice-based study, developed in a Brazilian research and development institute that works like a software house, asks how accessibility guidelines are handled in practice, how that compares with the market, and what it means for trust between products and the people who use them.

I was the accessibility ambassador at the company, driving the effort to create an accessibility baseline for all of its digital projects.

The work moved through four steps: map current practice with a survey, read the maturity it revealed, build a checklist around WCAG 2.2 Level A, and audit a pilot project with it.

The Problem

The institute works on a project-based model, serving clients in agribusiness, healthcare, logistics, and more. Accessibility appeared in its ESG reports under Diversity, Equity and Inclusion, through actions such as sign language interpreters and video captions, but it stayed socially framed and technically undefined: no dedicated goals, no audit indicators, no reference to technical standards for digital solutions.

That matters because users rarely see how a product was built, so they read the interface itself. An interface that fails basic criteria signals that some people who could use it were not considered, which erodes trust in the institution behind it. Around 1.3 billion people, about 16% of the global population, live with significant disability (WHO, 2023).

Research & Discovery

I started by mapping practice with an 11-item questionnaire on the technical stack, design system, and accessibility tools. It went to 58 active projects and 42 answered (72%) in June 2026. The same pattern showed up in every dimension: formal practice was almost entirely absent, even where the infrastructure already existed.

  • 79% had no accessibility tool, or didn’t know whether they did.
  • 74% applied no WCAG level, or didn’t know; only 4 of 42 reported Level AA.
  • No project ran automated accessibility testing, and 41 of 42 had never been audited.
  • 81% already had a CI/CD pipeline, but none included accessibility checks.

The market looks similar. Two Brazilian and Portuguese surveys show broad recognition (51% name inclusion of people with disabilities as a focus) but thinner practice: compliance checking tends to be reactive, and only about a quarter report regular evaluations by specialists. The gap is less about doing accessibility work than about verifying it.

The Checklist

Because maturity was low, advanced criteria would not have stuck. I proposed WCAG 2.2 Level A as the baseline for every project: the most fundamental requirements, organized by the POUR principles (Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust), with documented techniques that also help close the knowledge gap.

On top of it we built a checklist tailored to the product pipeline, so designers, developers, and QA each have their own criteria for any new feature, from design through test validation. The UI/UX checklist has 27 items, for example:

  • Every image, icon, or captcha has alternative text.
  • Color is never the only indicator of error, status, or action.
  • Interactive elements are keyboard-accessible, with visible focus and a logical tab order.
  • Form fields have clear labels and instructions, and errors use color, icon, and text.
  • Non-applicable items are marked N/A with a justification.

Pilot Audit

We applied the UI/UX checklist to a pilot project in the middle of migrating its Design System from version 1 to 2, across 59 prototype screens. The evaluation was AI-assisted and its results are treated as provisional while human review continues. It produced 912 audited occurrences:

  • 339 failed (37.2%)
  • 289 need attention (31.7%), partial compliance or a pending design decision
  • 134 flagged as dev requirements (14.7%)
  • 90 compliant (9.9%) and 60 not applicable (6.6%)

The most useful result: 51.3% of the failures came from the Design System itself or from using its components incorrectly. Those are fixable once in the shared library instead of screen by screen, so the problem is less about isolated screen-level decisions than about gaps in the component library and how it is used.

Impact & Learnings

  • A shared, auditable reference (WCAG 2.2 Level A) and a checklist that make accessibility expected and verifiable across teams.
  • Evidence that a first-pass AI-assisted audit is feasible, as long as it stays under human review.
  • A clear next path: with the checklist already integrated into company processes, audit projects that never had one, and add automated accessibility checks to the CI/CD pipelines that already exist.

Key takeaway: WCAG is not only a technical checklist. It is a design act of trust-building, because trust does not come from having standards or policies, it comes from the everyday, verifiable practice those standards produce.
