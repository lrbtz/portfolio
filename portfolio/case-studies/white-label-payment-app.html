White-Label Payment App

Vendors that sell through card terminals need a payment app they can launch under their own brand. I designed the MVP of a white-label payment app, built first for Venturus, covering selling, refunds, transactions, and corner cases in between.

Overview

This app lets merchants take payments on a card terminal. They enter an amount, choose how the customer pays (credit, debit, Pix, or voucher), complete the payment, and print the receipt. They can also look up past sales and refund them.

It was designed for Venturus as an MVP, and as the base that other vendors can adopt with their own visual identity. That second goal shaped every decision: the structure and behavior had to stay the same while the brand on top could change.

The Challenge

Taking a payment looks simple, but the screen is used at a counter, in a hurry, often with a customer waiting. Any hesitation, hidden fee, or unclear error costs time and trust.

There was a second layer: the product had to work for several vendors. A design tied to one brand would have to be rebuilt for each, so the challenge was to separate what is functional (flows, states, rules, copy) from what is brand (logo, accent color, illustration).

  • One task per screen. Large type, large touch targets, and a single clear action at the bottom.
  • Money is always explicit. The amount, any fees, and what the merchant receives are shown before confirming.
  • Every failure has a way forward. No dead ends: each error state offers a next step.
  • Brand-ready by design. Brand-driven elements are kept apart from functional colors such as confirm green and error red.

Users & Context

One product, many contexts. Merchants use it as independent sellers, cashiers, and delivery drivers, including older people and people with little tech familiarity, in bright sun, noise, queues, and with busy, wet, or gloved hands. The goal was the same everywhere: sell fast, without errors, and without training. Each profile or situation shaped a specific design response:

  • Little tech familiarity. Short text, one action per screen.
  • Older users and low vision. Large type, high contrast, wide buttons.
  • Bright sun, outdoors. Light background and dark text.
  • Noisy environment. Feedback that is 100% visual and redundant.
  • Busy or wet hands. Large targets, actions at the bottom.
  • High traffic (bakery, shop). Short flow and an immediate "New sale".
  • The end customer. Amount repeated, clear instructions, errors without embarrassment.
  • Color blindness. Never color alone: always an icon and text.

Approach

The MVP had a two-week design window, so the process had to be focused. I started by benchmarking what other companies in the payments space were doing for selling, installments, and refunds, to see which patterns merchants already know.

That let me spend the limited time where it mattered most: the rules, the states around the happy path, and a structure that could carry another vendor's brand.

Status & Learnings

  • The MVP design is complete across selling, refunds, transactions, and corner cases. It has not launched yet, so there are no usage results to report.
  • The structure is meant to be reused: a new vendor brings a logo, an accent color, and an illustration, and the flows and rules stay as they are.

Key takeaway: In a payment product, the happy path is the easy part. The design work was in the states around it: limits, failures, and irreversible actions. Separating what is functional from what is brand early on is what makes the same app usable by more than one company. Just as important is the user and the context of use: merchants, cashiers, delivery drivers, and older people, often with little tech familiarity, in bright sun, noise, queues, or with wet or busy hands. Every decision started from who is using the app and where.
