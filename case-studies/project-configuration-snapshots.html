Formulation Snapshots

Bakery teams had no way to preserve or compare a recipe's past formulation, once it was tweaked, the history was gone. I designed an end-to-end snapshot system that freezes a formulation in time, so users can compare recipes.

Overview

Proofline, a fictional platform standing in for the real, confidential product, tracked formulation records (a recipe as produced at a specific bakery site), along with the components attached to them (dough, filling, glaze), each with its own ingredients, quantities, and bake parameters. R&D, food safety, and purchasing teams used it to manage products through their lifecycle.

Snapshots are one feature within a much larger system; this case focuses only on that feature.

The Problem

As a formulation moved through its lifecycle, test kitchen → pilot bakery → full production, its components were edited continuously by multiple teams. But the system only ever showed the current state, once something changed, the prior state was gone.

That created a real operational gap. Teams had no way to answer basic questions like “What did this recipe actually look like when we approved it for pilot?” or “What changed between pilot and production?” without digging through change logs, memory, or photos of paper notes. Food safety audits, quality investigations, and cross-stage comparisons were slow and unreliable, there was no durable, trustworthy record of the past.

Research & Discovery

Working with the feature's product owner, I defined what a “snapshot” needed to guarantee to actually solve the problem, not just capture data, but be trusted as evidence. Discovery surfaced four hard constraints:

  • Immutability is non-negotiable. Once created, a snapshot can't be edited in any way, the moment historical data can change, it stops being evidence.
  • Creation needed to be asynchronous. Large formulations with many components could take time to snapshot; the flow had to support a clear Processing → Ready state with a completion notification, rather than blocking the user.
  • Ownership prevents accidental loss. Only the creator of a snapshot can delete it, protecting other teams' historical records from being wiped by someone outside that context.
  • Snapshots had to stay useful, not just archival. Teams wanted to actively compare a snapshot against a live formulation, or against another snapshot, and pull historical values back into the current recipe, not just view a frozen record.

Iterative Design

I mapped the full snapshot lifecycle end-to-end before touching visuals, because the hardest problems here were logical, not visual.

  • Access & management: a dedicated “Manage Snapshots” surface off the main formulations navigation, listing every snapshot with creator, creation date, component count, and status.
  • Creation flow: a modal for selecting product, bakery site, and lifecycle stage, then choosing which components to include, with an auto-generated but editable snapshot name.
  • Read states that read as frozen: Formulation and component detail views needed to communicate “you're looking at history” both visually and functionally, so I added a persistent banner (snapshot name + capture timestamp) and disabled every edit affordance across those views.
  • Comparison logic: the hardest part. The existing comparison tool only supported live-vs-live. I extended it into three distinct states instead of building three separate tools:
    • Live vs. Live: existing, fully editable behavior.
    • Live vs. Snapshot: read-only on the snapshot side, but users can copy values from the snapshot into the live formulation.
    • Snapshot vs. Snapshot: fully read-only, since there's no live formulation to write back to; the only action left is drilling into a component-level comparison.
  • Component-level comparison mirrored that same logic one level down, users can diff a snapshot component against its live counterpart and selectively “match” (copy) values across, such as an ingredient quantity or bake time, with a preview of exactly what would change before confirming.

Testing & Solution

Rather than a formal usability testing round, validation here happened through business-rule review with the product owner and implementation fidelity, the annotated flows and explicit rules meant engineering built the three comparison states as one coherent system, with no ambiguity about edge cases like what happens when both sides of a comparison are snapshots.

Impact & Learnings

  • Gave R&D and food safety teams a reliable way to freeze and later audit formulations, closing a real traceability gap.
  • Designing three comparison states (live/live, live/snapshot, snapshot/snapshot) as one system with conditional states, instead of three separate features, kept the interaction model predictable for users and kept engineering scope contained to a single comparison component.

Key takeaway: The main problem was deciding where the snapshot feature would live. The product owner wanted it inside the existing flow of the system, not as a separate entity. I built a quick mockup to show stakeholders that this wouldn't work well with the amount of information the page already had, and that a separate module would serve users best. A separate module gave snapshots, comparison, and the read-only views room to work as one predictable system, instead of squeezing them into a page that was already full.
